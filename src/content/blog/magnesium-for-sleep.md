Magnesium has become one of the most popular "sleep supplements" on social media. Some people swear by it; others notice nothing. So what's actually going on?

## What magnesium does in the body

Magnesium is a mineral involved in hundreds of processes, including muscle and nerve function, energy production, and keeping bones healthy. It also plays a part in regulating the nervous system, which is why researchers have looked at whether it might help with relaxation and sleep.

In the UK, adult men need about **300mg** a day and women about **270mg**.

## Does it help you sleep?

The honest answer: **possibly, for some people, but the evidence is weaker than marketing suggests.**

- Several small studies, mostly in older adults, have found modest improvements in how quickly people fell asleep or how they rated their sleep.
- Many of these studies were short, had few participants, or included people who were low in magnesium to begin with.
- Systematic reviews have generally concluded that the quality of evidence is low and that larger, better trials are needed.

What this likely means in practice: if your diet is low in magnesium, correcting that may help you feel better overall, including your sleep. If your intake is already adequate, a supplement is unlikely to be a game-changer.

## Food first

Most people can meet their needs through a varied diet. Good sources include:

| Food | Typical portion |
| --- | --- |
| Pumpkin seeds | A small handful (30g) |
| Almonds or cashews | A small handful (30g) |
| Spinach, cooked | 3 heaped tablespoons |
| Black beans or lentils | Half a tin |
| Wholemeal bread | 2 slices |
| Dark chocolate (70%+) | 2–3 squares |

Building a couple of these into each day, for example nuts or seeds on breakfast, beans at lunch and greens at dinner, covers most people comfortably.

## If you choose to try a supplement

Supplements come in several forms. Magnesium **citrate** and **glycinate** are generally better absorbed than magnesium **oxide**. Citrate can have a mild laxative effect, which some people find useful and others don't.

A few practical points:

- **Don't exceed 400mg a day** from supplements unless advised by a doctor. Higher doses commonly cause diarrhoea.
- Give it a fair trial of two to four weeks, and keep other sleep habits steady so you can tell whether it's helping.
- Treat it as a small addition to good sleep habits, not a replacement for them. Read our guide on [how to fall asleep faster](/blog/how-to-fall-asleep-faster/) for the habits that make the biggest difference.

## Who should be careful

Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before taking magnesium if you:

- have kidney disease, as your body may not clear excess magnesium
- take certain antibiotics, osteoporosis medicines (bisphosphonates), or medicines for reflux, as magnesium can affect how they're absorbed
- take diuretics or other medicines that change mineral levels
- are pregnant or breastfeeding

## The bottom line

Magnesium is an essential nutrient, and eating enough of it supports good health. The evidence that supplements improve sleep in otherwise healthy people is limited. Start with food, focus on the habits that reliably improve sleep, and if you do try a supplement, keep to a sensible dose and check with a professional if you have any health conditions.
