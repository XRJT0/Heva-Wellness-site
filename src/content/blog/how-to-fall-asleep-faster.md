Lying awake watching the clock is one of the most frustrating parts of modern life. The good news is that, for most people, falling asleep faster isn't about willpower. It's about giving your body clear, consistent signals that it's time to rest.

Below are nine habits that sleep clinicians recommend most often. You don't need to adopt all of them at once. Pick two or three that fit your life and give them two weeks.

## 1. Wake up at the same time every day

It sounds backwards, but a fixed **wake-up** time is the single most useful anchor for your sleep. Your body clock (circadian rhythm) sets itself largely from when you wake and see light. Keep the time within about 30 minutes, including weekends, and your body will start to feel sleepy at a predictable time in the evening.

## 2. Get daylight in the morning

Bright light in the first hour after waking tells your brain that the day has started. That, in turn, helps it release melatonin at the right time later on. Ten to twenty minutes outdoors, even on a cloudy day, gives you far more light than a well-lit office.

## 3. Time your caffeine

Caffeine blocks adenosine, a chemical that builds up during the day and makes you feel sleepy. It also lingers: a large share of an afternoon coffee can still be in your system at bedtime. If you have trouble falling asleep, try a **2pm cut-off** for coffee, tea, energy drinks and cola for two weeks and see what changes.

## 4. Keep alcohol modest and early

A drink can make you feel drowsy, but alcohol fragments sleep later in the night and reduces the deeper, more restorative stages. If you drink, have it earlier in the evening and leave a few hours before bed.

## 5. Build a 30-minute wind-down

Your brain needs a runway. A short routine that you repeat every night becomes a cue for sleep. It might be:

- dimming the lights and putting your phone on charge outside the bedroom
- a warm shower or bath
- light stretching or a few minutes of slow breathing
- reading something on paper

The specific activities matter less than doing them in the same order each night.

## 6. Make the bedroom cool, dark and quiet

Most people sleep best in a slightly cool room, around 16–19°C. Blackout curtains or an eye mask help in light summer evenings, and earplugs or a fan can mask background noise.

## 7. Keep the bed for sleep

If you regularly work, scroll or worry in bed, your brain starts to link the bed with being alert. Try to use it only for sleep and intimacy, so getting into bed becomes a strong signal to switch off.

## 8. Don't fight it: get up after 20 minutes

If you've been lying awake for what feels like 20 minutes, get up, go to another dimly lit room and do something calm until you feel sleepy, then return to bed. Don't check the time repeatedly; clock-watching tends to increase anxiety about sleep. This approach, called stimulus control, is a core part of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).

## 9. Park your worries before bed

A racing mind is one of the most common reasons people can't fall asleep. Earlier in the evening, spend five minutes writing down what's on your mind and one next step for each item. It gives your brain permission to stop rehearsing them at midnight.

## When to see a doctor

Occasional bad nights are normal. Speak to your GP if:

- you've had trouble sleeping for three months or more
- poor sleep is affecting your mood, work or relationships
- a partner notices loud snoring, gasping or pauses in your breathing
- you feel very sleepy during the day even after a full night in bed

Persistent insomnia is very treatable, and CBT-I is the recommended first-line treatment in the UK, ahead of sleeping tablets.

## The short version

Wake at the same time, get morning light, stop caffeine early, give yourself a wind-down routine, and get up if you can't sleep. Give these habits two weeks before judging them; sleep tends to improve gradually rather than overnight.
