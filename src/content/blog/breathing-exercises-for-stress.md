When you're stressed, your breathing tends to become fast and shallow, high up in the chest. That's part of the body's alarm response, and it can feed the feeling that something is wrong. Slowing your breath down is one of the quickest ways to send the opposite signal.

The three exercises below take a few minutes, need no equipment, and can be done sitting at a desk, on a train, or lying in bed.

## Before you start

- Sit or lie comfortably, with your shoulders relaxed.
- Breathe through your nose if you can.
- Let your belly rise as you breathe in, rather than lifting your shoulders.
- Never force your breath. If you feel dizzy or light-headed, go back to normal breathing.

## 1. Calm breathing

This is the simplest technique and a good place to begin. It's similar to the approach recommended by the NHS.

1. Breathe in gently through your nose, counting steadily from one to four.
2. Without pausing, breathe out gently through your mouth, counting from one to five or six.
3. Repeat for three to five minutes.

**Best for:** everyday tension, winding down before sleep, or as a daily practice.

## 2. Box breathing

Box breathing adds short holds, which gives your mind something to focus on. It's popular with people who find their thoughts racing.

1. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four.
2. Hold your breath gently for four.
3. Breathe out through your mouth for four.
4. Hold for four.
5. Repeat four to six times.

If four feels too long, use three. The rhythm matters more than the number.

**Best for:** before a presentation, an exam or a difficult conversation.

## 3. Extended exhale

Making your out-breath noticeably longer than your in-breath is one of the most reliable ways to slow your heart rate.

1. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four.
2. Breathe out slowly through slightly pursed lips, as if blowing through a straw, for a count of six to eight.
3. Repeat for two to three minutes.

**Best for:** moments of acute stress or a rising sense of panic.

## Make it a habit

Breathing techniques work best when they feel familiar. Try practising one exercise for two to three minutes, once or twice a day, when you're already fairly calm, such as after brushing your teeth or before your first coffee. Then, when stress hits, your body already knows what to do.

You can also link breathing to habits you already have. A few slow breaths after a [post-meal walk](/blog/walking-after-meals/) or as part of a [bedtime wind-down](/blog/how-to-fall-asleep-faster/) fit naturally.

## When breathing isn't enough

Breathing exercises are a useful tool, but they're not a treatment for anxiety disorders or depression. Speak to your GP if:

- you feel anxious or low most days for two weeks or more
- worry or panic is stopping you doing everyday things
- you're having panic attacks

In the UK you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies in most areas. If you're in crisis or feel unsafe, call 999, or contact Samaritans any time on 116 123.

## The bottom line

Slow, steady breathing with a longer out-breath is a simple, free way to take the edge off stress. Choose one exercise, practise it daily for a week, and keep it in your pocket for the moments you need it most.
